Chapter 05 · Transport networks

Slime that builds networks

A yellow film creeps across a dish of oat flakes. It has no brain and no nerves. Within a day or so it has joined the flakes with a web of tubes that an engineer would not be embarrassed by.

The film is Physarum polycephalum, usually called a slime mould. It is not a mould, or any kind of fungus. In the stage people study, the plasmodium, it is a single cell: one enormous bag of cytoplasm holding many nuclei, with no walls between them. A plasmodium can spread over many centimetres. It feeds by engulfing microbes, and in the lab it is happy on oats.

Inside, the cell is a network of tubes. Cytoplasm streams through them, sloshing back and forth and reversing direction every minute or two. Tubes that carry a lot of flow tend to thicken. Tubes that carry little thin out and are eventually abandoned. Nothing in the cell surveys the whole network and decides which tubes to keep. Each bit of tube responds only to what is flowing through it.

A maze, carefully stated

In 2000 Toshiyuki Nakagaki, Hiroyasu Yamada and Ágota Tóth published a one-page paper in Nature with the title "Maze-solving by an amoeboid organism." They cut a small maze out of plastic film laid on agar and filled every corridor with pieces of plasmodium, which fused into one cell. Then they put food at two exits.

Over the next several hours the cell pulled its tubes out of the dead ends and the long way round, and what remained was a thick tube joining the two food sources through the maze, usually along the shortest route.

It is worth being precise about what that means. The slime did not wander in from the entrance and search for the exit, the way a person or a mouse would. It already filled the whole maze. What it did was prune: start with every path at once and let the poorly used ones wither. That is still remarkable for a cell with no nervous system, and it is a genuinely useful way to find short paths. It is just a different trick from the one the word "solve" calls to mind.

The rule

In 2010 Jeff Jones, a computer scientist, published a model that grows Physarum-like networks out of a crowd of particles. It does not simulate cytoplasm, tubes or flow. It is closer in spirit to an ant colony: lots of simple agents, each leaving a chemical trail and following the trails of others.

The world is a grid holding a trail value in every square. Each agent has a position and a heading, and three sensors that sit a fixed distance ahead of it: one straight ahead, one angled to the left, one angled to the right. Every step, each agent does four things:

Sense the trail under its three sensors. Turn toward the strongest: if the forward sensor is strongest, keep straight; if left or right is stronger, rotate that way by a fixed turn angle; if forward is the weakest of the three, turn left or right at random. Move one square forward. Deposit a little trail where it lands.

Then the trail map itself does two things: it diffuses, each square becoming the average of itself and its eight neighbours, and it decays, losing a fixed fraction everywhere. That is the whole model.

No agent knows where any network is. It knows three numbers. But every agent that follows a trail also adds to it, so busy trails get busier and quiet ones fade. Below, tens of thousands of agents start scattered at random and do exactly this.

What the dials do

Within a few seconds the scattered agents gather into strands, and the strands join into a lacy network of cords and cells.

Press Jones 2010 to run the model with the values from Jones's paper. The lace doesn't last. Small loops close up, thin strands drain into thicker ones, and after a couple of thousand steps only a few fat cords are left. That is the model's real behaviour, and it echoes the organism, which also abandons most of its tubes and keeps a few. But it makes for a dull dish, so the other presets add one dial of our own that is not in Jones's model: wobble, a small random jitter added to each agent's heading every step. A little jitter keeps agents spilling out of the big cords, and with about 25° of it the network settles into a mesh of cells that stays a mesh.

The other dials change the mesh's character. Jones explored how the patterns depend on the parameters and found that the sensor angle, the turn angle and how far ahead the sensors reach matter most. A short sensor distance gives a fine, crowded mesh (Fine mesh). A long one lets agents notice trails from far away, so the network is made of fewer, longer cords (Coarse cords). Decay sets how long a trail is remembered: low decay lets old trails linger and helps the network coarsen, high decay forgets quickly. Turn the wobble down to zero on any preset and watch it start to prune itself; turn it all the way up and the agents are too jittery to follow anything.

Notice what the model leaves out. There is no tube, no thickness, no flow and no food in the rule. A "cord" is just a place where many agents happen to be following one another. The network is a pattern in the crowd, not a structure anyone built.

The Tokyo experiment

A maze has one right answer. Most real networks don't. A rail system, for instance, is pulled between three goals that fight each other. It should be cheap, which means short total track. It should be efficient, which means short trips between any two stations. And it should be robust, which means that cutting one link should not strand anyone. A tree is cheapest but fragile. Connecting everything to everything is efficient and robust but absurdly expensive.

In 2010 Atsushi Tero, Nakagaki and colleagues published a test of how Physarum handles that trade-off. They placed oat flakes on a wet surface in the pattern of cities around Tokyo, with the slime starting at the flake for Tokyo itself. Because Physarum avoids light, they used lighting to mark mountains and coastline where it should not grow. The plasmodium spread out, and then, as in the maze, it thinned its tubes down to a network linking the flakes.

They compared those networks with the real rail network of the region on the three measures above: total length, travel efficiency and how well the network survived losing a random link. On those measures the slime's networks were comparable to the railway. Some came out a little better on one measure and a little worse on another, as you would expect from a design that trades them off. The paper also introduced a mathematical model of the tubes based on flow, in which tubes carrying more flux grow thicker. That model, not the agent model on this page, is the one built to explain the experiment.

The agents start everywhere at once, the way the plasmodium filled the maze. Wherever food sits, trail is always strong, so agents gather there, and the cords that form between nearby sources are reinforced from both ends. Move a source and watch the cords that ran to it slacken while new ones grow toward its new position. Remove one and its links fade within seconds. Remove all of them and the network forgets the layout and goes back to the free-floating pattern of Figure 2.

The honest caveats

Jones's model is an approximation of the look of a Physarum network, not of its biology. It reproduces the way strands form, merge and coarsen, and it is fast and easy to play with, but its agents are not pieces of a cell and its trail is not cytoplasm. A model that grows networks like Physarum does not prove that Physarum works like the model. This version also differs from Jones's original in two ways: it leaves out his rule that no two agents may share a grid square, and, except in the Jones 2010 preset, it adds the wobble described above, which is our own change to stop the network coarsening.

"Solves mazes" is a fair headline only with the asterisk from earlier. The organism started out occupying the whole maze and kept the best-supplied route. Later work has shown it can do surprising things when it really does have to explore, but the 2000 result is about pruning.

The Tokyo comparison was made on specific measures: length, efficiency and fault tolerance. It does not mean that a slime mould could plan a railway. Real rail networks answer to terrain, land prices, history and politics, none of which an oat flake represents. What the experiment showed is narrower and still striking: a feedback rule that thickens busy tubes and starves idle ones finds good compromises between cost, speed and robustness, with no one weighing them up.

Five rules

That is the last chapter. Each one has been a different version of the same small surprise. Sand grains that topple at four built a pile that tunes itself to the edge of collapse. Birds that steer by their nearest neighbours made a flock that turns as one. Chemicals that react and spread at different speeds laid down spots and stripes. An ant that turns left or right on the colour of its square wandered for ten thousand steps and then built a highway. And a crowd of particles that follow the strongest scent drew a network linking every source of food. In none of them does any part see the whole, and in all of them the whole has a shape. That gap between what each piece knows and what the pieces make together is what this field guide has been about.
