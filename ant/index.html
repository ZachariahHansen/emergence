Chapter 04 · Order after chaos

The ant and the highway

An ant on a grid follows a rule you can learn in ten seconds. For ten thousand steps it makes a mess. Then it starts building a road, and it never stops.

In 1986 Chris Langton, a computer scientist who was trying to work out what "artificial life" might mean, described a handful of tiny machines living on grids. One of them is now called Langton's ant. It is about as simple as a program can be and still do something interesting, and forty years later it still holds a question nobody has answered.

The rule

Take a grid of squares, all white. Put an ant on one square, facing up. At each step the ant looks at the square it is standing on:

  • On a white square, it turns 90° to the right.
  • On a black square, it turns 90° to the left.

Then it flips the colour of that square, white to black or black to white, and moves forward one square. That's all. There is no randomness anywhere. Run it twice from the same start and you get exactly the same thing twice.

Three acts

Simple. For the first few hundred steps the ant draws small, tidy shapes, often with an obvious symmetry. If you step through them one at a time you can follow its logic and predict the next move without much trouble.

Chaos. Then the tidiness goes. The ant builds a ragged, growing blob with no structure you can see. It doubles back over its own trail, erases parts of it, and wanders. This goes on for about ten thousand steps. Nothing about the blob tells you it is about to change.

The highway. After about ten thousand steps (9,977, to be exact) the ant falls into a cycle. It makes a sequence of 104 moves that leaves it two squares further along a diagonal, facing the same way, with the same pattern of black squares around it, just shifted. Then it does the same 104 moves again. And again. The trail it leaves behind is a striped diagonal band that people call the highway.

On an infinite empty grid, the highway goes on forever. That part is not a guess: each 104-step cycle only ever runs into fresh white squares, and from fresh white squares it does exactly what it did last time. The marker in Figure 1 shows where the cycle started. The figure finds it by checking, as the ant runs, whether its last few hundred moves were the moves of 104 steps earlier shifted by a constant amount; it raises the marker once that has held for four full cycles.

The rule says nothing about diagonals, cycles or roads. All of that comes out of the walking.

What is proven, and what isn't

You might guess that the ant could, from some starting pattern, get stuck forever going round in a small region. It can't. In 1992 Leonid Bunimovich and Serge Troubetzkoy proved that, whatever squares start out black, the ant's path is unbounded: it always eventually travels arbitrarily far from where it started. The proof is short and uses a neat fact about the rule. Since the ant turns on every single step, its moves alternate between horizontal and vertical, which constrains how it can enter each square enough to rule out staying trapped.

But "it goes arbitrarily far" is much weaker than "it builds a highway." The path could, in principle, sprawl outward in some messy way forever. Whether every finite starting pattern eventually leads to a highway is an open problem. Every finite pattern anyone has tried has ended in the same 104-step highway. Nobody has a proof that they all do, and nobody has found one that doesn't.

You can add to the experiments. Paint black squares onto the grid below, before or during a run, or start from a patch of random noise.

The time it takes varies wildly. A random patch might give a highway after two thousand steps or after forty thousand. A highway that runs into something you painted usually breaks, and the ant goes back to chaos for a while, and then, as far as anyone has seen, it builds another one somewhere else. If you find a pattern on which the ant runs into the edge without ever settling down, that is not a counterexample. The grid is only 160 squares wide, and the conjecture is about an infinite one.

More colours

There's an obvious way to generalise the ant. Give squares more than two colours, cycling through them in order, and give each colour its own turn. Write the rule as a string of letters, one per colour: Langton's ant is RL, "turn right on colour 0, left on colour 1." The rule LLRR has four colours, and so on. Greg Turk and Jim Propp both explored this family, and these creatures are often called turmites.

The family is extremely varied. Some rules grow patterns with perfect symmetry. Some produce chaos that, as far as anyone has run them, never resolves. Some build highways of their own, with different shapes and much longer periods than 104. You can't tell which by looking at the letters.

A few to try. LLRR grows a shape that keeps getting bigger and keeps returning, over and over, to exact mirror symmetry. RLR just grows a chaotic blob. LRRRRRLLR fills in a square around itself. RRLLLRLLLRRR builds a solid triangle that crawls across the grid as it grows. LLRRRLRLRLLR spends about thirty thousand steps in chaos and then builds a broad, intricate highway that, in this simulation, repeats every 1,968 steps.

You have to run it

Here's the part worth sitting with. You know everything about Langton's ant. The rule fits on a sticky note and there is no hidden state, no noise, nothing left out. And still, if someone asks "when does the highway start?", the only known way to answer is to run the ant for 9,977 steps and watch. Nobody knows a formula that takes the rule and hands you that number. Change one square of the starting pattern and the number usually changes, in no way you can foresee.

This is a different kind of unpredictability from the sandpile's. The sandpile is unpredictable because it is huge and poised on the edge of collapse. The ant is unpredictable even though it is one creature following one rule, because what it does next depends on everything it has done before. It is not only that we haven't found the shortcut yet. For the ant there is some evidence that no general shortcut exists: in 2002 Anahí Gajardo, Andrés Moreira and Eric Goles showed that, given a suitably prepared starting grid, the ant's trail can be made to compute any Boolean logic circuit. Predicting the ant in general is therefore at least as hard as predicting what an arbitrary circuit will output.

Stephen Wolfram calls this kind of situation computational irreducibility: a process whose outcome can't be found any faster than by carrying the process out. It's usually very hard to prove that a particular system is irreducible in that strong sense, and nobody has proved it for the ant. But the ant is the cleanest illustration of the idea there is. Knowing the rule completely and knowing what it will do are two different things.

The honest caveats

Every grid here is finite and the ant stops at the edge, so these figures show the start of a story the mathematics tells about an infinite grid. The highway detector is a check on the ant's recent moves, not a proof, and it only knows about the 104-step highway. The step 9,977 is for an empty grid; on any other start the onset is usually different, and the highway can point along any of the four diagonals.

And the central claim of the chapter is an observation, not a theorem. In every experiment anyone has published, the ant ends up on a highway. It might be true for every finite start. It might fail on some pattern nobody has tried. For a rule this simple, it is remarkable that we don't know.
